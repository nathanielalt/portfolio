Tabletop Wind Tunnel Ongoing

From CAD to visible flow: a compact, 3D-printed tunnel with a custom smoke-delivery system.

Project at a Glance

Independent Design and Fabrication
Fusion 360 · FDM 3D Printing · Experimental Flow Visualization

The Problem

Build a compact tabletop wind tunnel that produces visible smoke streaks while keeping the structure, airflow path, and smoke source practical to fabricate and modify.

What I Developed

  • Modelled a modular inlet, honeycomb, test section, and downstream diffuser in Fusion 360.
  • Fabricated PLA components using a Bambu Lab A1 mini, iterating on wall thickness and printable geometry.
  • Developed a smoke chamber, injection rake, and return tubing that diverts part of the fan exhaust into the smoke supply.
Key Result

Visible smoke streaks achieved during testing. Iteration improved tracer delivery, while repeatability, chamber sealing, and heat management remain active development areas.

01 · Airflow and Mechanical Design

A Compact Suction Tunnel

A downstream fan draws air through a shaped inlet and honeycomb into the test section. The inlet contracts toward the viewing region, while the honeycomb is intended to reduce transverse flow. The downstream diffuser expands the passage toward the fan.

The test-section height is approximately 80 mm. The compact layout balances viewing access, printable component sizes, and the space needed for the inlet and diffuser.

Fan-Assisted Smoke Delivery

Part of the fan exhaust is routed back to the incense chamber to help carry smoke into the injection system. The main tunnel remains open to the room: this is an open-circuit tunnel with a partial exhaust return for smoke delivery, rather than a fully closed-return wind tunnel.

The smoke rake was revised to approximately 12 outlets, each 1 mm in diameter. Testing focused on balancing smoke production, delivery resistance, leakage, and dilution by the main airflow.

CAD to Printed Hardware

I used Fusion 360 to develop the tunnel sections, patterned honeycomb, chamber, and connecting tubes. The CAD/CAM workflow centred on modelling, slicing, print preparation, and FDM fabrication. Nominal wall thickness was reduced from 5 mm to 3 mm to limit material use while retaining a practical structure.

Separating the tunnel into printed components made it possible to revise smoke routing and interfaces as test results exposed problems. Fit, sealing, material consumption, and the thermal limits of PLA became design constraints alongside the airflow geometry.

03 · Challenges + Solutions

Smoke Pooling and Weak Delivery

Early tests produced smoke in the source chamber without consistently carrying it into the viewing section. Opening the lid changed both ember intensity and smoke transport, indicating that the chamber airflow mattered as much as smoke production. I revised the delivery path and incorporated the fan-exhaust feed to help move smoke toward the rake.

Inconsistent Smoke Streaks

Smoke leaving a tube did not always produce distinct streaks in the test section. I investigated rake openings, gaps around the injection assembly, filter resistance, and the balance between fan flow and smoke supply. Some tests produced clear visualization; repeatability remains a focus.

Incense Orientation and Supply

Backflow cones initially allowed smoke to collect low in the chamber. Breaking up a cone improved upward transport, and later tests showed that vertically held incense sticks produced more useful smoke than the earlier arrangement. A holder for multiple upright sticks was identified as a next iteration for a more consistent supply.

Heat Distortion and Lid Leakage

The intense heat from cone incense warped the plastic, while an imperfect lid seal allowed smoke to escape. These tests exposed the need to improve thermal separation around the source and the chamber closure. Lower-intensity stick incense is part of the refinement direction; a durable heat-management and sealing solution remains to be verified.

04 · Results and Next Steps

What the Prototype Established

  • Translated a modular Fusion 360 assembly into printed tunnel hardware.
  • Achieved visible smoke streaks in the test section during successful trials.
  • Identified chamber airflow, incense orientation, and delivery-path losses as practical influences on visualization.
  • Used test observations to guide changes to the smoke circuit and printed components.

What Comes Next

  • Improve smoke consistency with controlled source positioning and supply.
  • Resolve chamber heat distortion and lid leakage, then repeat visualization trials.
  • Document the assembled prototype and smoke flow using the media spaces below.

Results are qualitative. Airspeed, flow uniformity, and turbulence intensity have not been quantified for this tabletop build.
