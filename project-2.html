CanSat Autonomous Glider

The Problem

Develop a compact, rocket-deployed autonomous glider and integrate its aerodynamic surfaces, structure, and mechanisms.

My Contributions

  • Proposed the selected lifting-body fuselage and screened more than 20 airfoil profiles.
  • Refined the fuselage using CFD, vortex generators, and endplates.
  • Contributed to composite manufacture, tail components, and mechanical integration.
Key Result

Approximately 5× simulated fuselage lift versus the baseline without flow-control devices at 20 m/s.

Simulation result for the fuselage configuration; team competition results appear below.

Project overview

As part of the RSX Aerial Design Team, I was primarily responsible for the aerodynamic development of an autonomous glider for the AAS CanSat Competition within the mechanical subsystem. I proposed the lifting-body fuselage concept that was ultimately selected for the final vehicle and led much of its aerodynamic development, from early configuration studies through analysis and design refinement.

I developed a staged workflow that combined rapid, lower-fidelity analysis with more detailed CFD. I first used OpenVSP and VSPAERO to compare more than 20 candidate airfoil profiles and explore a range of fuselage geometries. The most promising configurations were then selected for higher-fidelity analysis in Autodesk CFD, with key results and flow trends also cross-checked using ANSYS Fluent.

The CFD results helped me examine pressure distributions, streamlines, wake development, and boundary-layer behaviour around the lifting-body fuselage. This analysis indicated premature flow separation and considerable spanwise and tip-flow losses, particularly at the glider’s low-Reynolds-number operating conditions. Based on the observed flow behaviour, I incorporated vortex generators and endplates as passive flow-control devices. In simulation, these changes significantly delayed separation and produced approximately five times the simulated lift of the baseline fuselage without those devices at the 20 m/s design speed.

Beyond the glider fuselage, I evaluated rocket nose-cone geometries using Autodesk CFD and OpenRocket, supported CFD-based verification of control mechanisms and aerodynamic interfaces, and assisted with the fibre-composite manufacture of the flight wings. I also played a major role in the refinement of individual parts for electronic and mechanical integration. A significant portion of the tail and release mechanisms were also manufactured and refined by me.

The lift comparison concerns the lifting-body fuselage configuration in simulation. It is not a measured fivefold increase in the lift of the complete aircraft. The CFD result informed geometry selection and refinement; experimental confirmation remains a separate step.

Challenges + Solutions

Premature flow separation

CFD indicated separation and spanwise losses around the lifting-body fuselage. I incorporated vortex generators and endplates and compared the modified geometry with the baseline, obtaining approximately five times the simulated fuselage lift at the 20 m/s design speed.

Efficient geometry selection

Evaluating every candidate with detailed CFD would have required substantial iteration time. I used OpenVSP and VSPAERO to screen more than 20 airfoil profiles, then investigated the most promising configurations in Autodesk CFD and cross-checked key flow trends in ANSYS Fluent.

Manufacturing and subsystem integration

The T-tail and camera integration required reconciling CAD assemblies with manufacturing tolerances and the needs of both the mechanical and electrical subsystems. I refined component clearances and interfaces to support reliable fit and straightforward assembly. For load-bearing printed components, I selected build orientations with the expected loading and weaker interlayer bonds in mind, reducing reliance on interlayer adhesion in critical load paths.
